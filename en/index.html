Shipping ports

The Canadian seaboards open onto three very different routes: the Pacific towards Asia, the St. Lawrence and the Atlantic towards Europe and North Africa, the Great Lakes towards the interior of the continent. The port of departure is not a logistics detail: it drives the lead time, the cost and sometimes the feasibility of the sale.

Standards and documents to provide

These are boxes to fill in, not certificates on display. None is presented as held: the page says what a buyer will ask for, not what the seller owns.

  • Forest certificationThe scheme under which the forest of origin is certified, and the type of claim attached to it. This is the first document a European or Japanese buyer asks for.
  • Structural gradingThe grading rules applied and the body supervising the stamp. Without it, the timber cannot be used structurally in the destination market.
  • Importing market conformity declarationEvery destination has its own placing-on-the-market regime for timber. The field exists to say which one applies, not to assert that it is satisfied.
  • Due diligence on originTraceability back to the forest of origin, required on import in several markets. Prepared before the order, not at customs clearance.
  • Phytosanitary treatment of packagingThe mark carried by pallets and cases. The most frequent blocking point on arrival, and the easiest to avoid.
  • Preservative treatmentThe product used and the intended use class. Permitted products differ from one country to another.

How to read this catalogue Every card is a product CATEGORY, not a reference in stock. It states what the category covers, the applications where it is called for, its level of processing, and the grid of fields an international buyer will require before quoting. That grid is what structures the catalogue: as soon as a real offer exists, it slots into a category and inherits its fields. The level of processing is an axis in its own right — the log buyer and the glulam buyer are not the same person and do not ask for the same documents.
What the page deliberately does not show The brief asked for supply capacities. There are none on this page, and that is deliberate: no volume, no tonnage, no lead time, no price, no shipping frequency, no certification presented as held, no species announced as available. I have measured nothing and no supply data has been provided to me. On an export page, an announced volume is the first thing a buyer has checked, and the first thing that kills a deal if it is wrong. So the page states which unit to declare and on what basis, never how much. The ports listed are real and given with their province and their seaboard only — facts verifiable in thirty seconds.
